Testicular Pain: When It's a Medical Emergency

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Sudden, severe testicular pain is a medical emergency, especially when paired with rapid swelling, redness, fever, nausea, or urinary changes. These symptoms can signal serious conditions such as testicular torsion or infection, both of which require immediate medical attention. Explanation

Testicular pain is common among males of all ages. In many cases, it's minor and resolves on its own. However, some causes require urgent medical attention. Knowing when to act can save your fertility, prevent complications, or even save your life.

Understanding Testicular Pain

Testicular pain refers to discomfort in one or both testicles, scrotum, or the groin area. Causes range from benign to serious. Mild ache after exercise or minor trauma often improves with rest and over-the-counter pain relief. But sudden, severe pain can signal a medical emergency.

Testicular pain can vary in onset, intensity, and duration.

Pain may radiate into the groin, lower abdomen, or flank.

Accompanying symptoms—swelling, redness, fever—help identify the cause.

When to Seek Emergency Care

Some signs indicate an urgent problem. If you experience any of these, go to the nearest emergency department or call emergency services immediately:

Sudden, severe testicular pain, especially with no obvious injury

Rapid scrotal swelling or redness

Nausea or vomiting accompanying the pain

Pain that wakes you from sleep

Pain radiating into the abdomen

Fever higher than 100.4°F (38°C)

Difficulty urinating or blood in the urine

History of undescended testicle or previous testicular surgery

Ignoring these symptoms may lead to irreversible damage, including loss of the testicle or reduced fertility.

Common Emergency Causes

1. Testicular Torsion

Occurs when the spermatic cord twists, cutting off blood flow.

Most common in adolescents but can happen at any age.

Presents with sudden, excruciating pain and scrotal swelling.

Requires surgery within 6 hours to save the testicle.

2. Torsion of the Appendix Testis

A small appendage on the testicle twists.

Causes sharp pain and a "blue dot" sign on the scrotum.

Less dangerous than full torsion but still very painful.

May resolve on its own, but evaluate with a healthcare provider.

3. Epididymitis and Orchitis

Infection or inflammation of the epididymis (tube at back of testicle) or testicle itself.

Commonly caused by bacteria or sexually transmitted infections.

Symptoms: gradual pain, swelling, tenderness, fever, urinary symptoms.

Treated with antibiotics, rest, scrotal support, and pain relief.

4. Trauma

Direct injury (sports, accidents) can bruise or fracture the testicle.

Severe impact may cause bleeding into the scrotum (hematocele).

Immediate evaluation rules out rupture or serious damage.

Inguinal hernia: part of the intestine pushes into the groin, causing pain.

Kidney stones: pain from the flank may radiate into the scrotum.

Varicocele or hydrocele: enlarged veins or fluid around the testicle, usually painless but can ache.

Diagnosing the Cause

A prompt, accurate diagnosis ensures the right treatment. In the emergency department, doctors typically:

Take a detailed medical history:

Onset, duration, and character of pain

Associated symptoms: fever, urinary changes, nausea

Recent injuries or sexual history

Perform a physical exam:

Examine the scrotum, testicles, groin, and abdomen

Check for swelling, tenderness, and any masses

Assess cremasteric reflex (stroking inner thigh causes testicle to rise)

Order tests as needed:

Doppler ultrasound: evaluates blood flow to the testicle

Urinalysis and urine culture: detect infection or blood

Blood tests: check for signs of infection or inflammation

Ultrasound is the gold standard to distinguish torsion (absent blood flow) from infection (increased blood flow).

Treatment Options

Testicular Torsion

[Ubie Doctor's](https://collagen-atelier.bbsxyxy.com/)

Emergency surgery (detorsion and orchiopexy) to untwist and secure the testicle.

If treated within 6 hours, the testicle can usually be saved.

Delays beyond 12 hours greatly increase risk of permanent damage.

Infections (Epididymitis/Orchitis)

Antibiotic therapy—often covering sexually transmitted bacteria or urinary pathogens.

Scrotal elevation and ice packs to reduce swelling.

NSAIDs (ibuprofen) for pain relief.

Trauma

Rest and scrotal support (jockstrap) to limit movement.

Ice packs and NSAIDs to control pain and swelling.

Surgical repair if there's testicular rupture or large hematocele.

Other Conditions

Hernia: surgical repair if incarcerated or strangulated.

Kidney stones: pain management, hydration, possible lithotripsy.

Varicocele/hydrocele: often monitored; surgery if painful or fertility is affected.

Self-Care and Monitoring

Even when pain is mild, it's important to track your symptoms:

Note when the pain began and any activity or injury that triggered it.

Watch for changes in pain intensity, location, or quality.

Monitor for new symptoms: fever, urinary issues, increased swelling.

Use scrotal support and over-the-counter NSAIDs for mild discomfort.

Avoid heavy lifting and strenuous exercise until pain resolves.

If self-care measures don't improve symptoms within 24–48 hours, or if pain worsens, contact your healthcare provider.

Preventing Future Problems

While not all causes of testicular pain can be prevented, you can reduce risk by:

Wearing protective gear during sports (athletic cup)

Practice safe sex to lower infection risk

Perform regular self-exams to detect lumps or abnormal swelling early

Seek prompt evaluation for any testicular changes or persistent discomfort

When to Talk to a Doctor

Testicular pain should never be ignored. Even if the pain seems mild or intermittent, a medical evaluation can rule out serious conditions. Speak to a doctor if you experience:

Persistent or recurrent testicular pain

Any lump, swelling, or changes in size

Pain associated with fever or urinary symptoms

Pain that interferes with daily activities or sleep

In cases of possible life-threatening conditions—like testicular torsion—every minute counts. If you're ever unsure, it's better to be seen right away.
