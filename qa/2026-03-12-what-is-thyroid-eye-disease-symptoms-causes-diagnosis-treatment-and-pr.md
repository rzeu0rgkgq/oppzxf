# What Is Thyroid Eye Disease? Symptoms, Causes, Diagnosis, Treatment, and Prevention

Thyroid eye disease (TED), also known as Graves’ ophthalmopathy or Graves’ orbitopathy, is an autoimmune disease that may affect the appearance and function of one or both eyes.

According to the American Thyroid Association, TED most often affects people with Graves’ disease, an autoimmune disease that causes hyperthyroidism, or an overproduction of thyroid hormones. But TED can also occur on its own, in people with normal or low thyroid hormone levels.

In thyroid eye disease, the immune system attacks the muscles and other soft tissues around the eyeballs, resulting in a spectrum of symptoms that may include a feeling of grittiness in the eyes, redness, eyelid malposition and swelling, sensitivity to light, bulging eyes, and blurred or even double vision, per the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK).

Signs and Symptoms of Thyroid Eye Disease

TED typically has an active disease phase, during which symptoms develop, that lasts from six months to two years, per the National Organization for Rare Diseases (NORD). This is followed by an inactive phase, during which the disease doesn’t progress, but some symptoms persist. Recurrence of TED has been considered quite rare, but some research suggests that it may be somewhat more common than has been previously thought.

Signs and symptoms of Graves’ ophthalmopathy may include:

Dry eyes

Watery eyes

A gritty sensation in the eyes

Red, irritated, or inflamed eyes

Sensitivity to light

Pain or pressure in the eyes

Puffy eyelids

Bulging eyes (called proptosis or exophthalmos)

The appearance of staring

Eyelids that retract

Difficulty fully closing the eyes

Double vision (diplopia)

Vision loss

Usually symptoms occur in both eyes, per the Cleveland Clinic, but they can occur in just one eye (except diplopia, which by definition involves both eyes, although one eye may be normal).

Causes of Thyroid Eye Disease

Thyroid eye disease is caused by an autoimmune attack — meaning the immune system targets the body’s own healthy cells — on muscle connective tissue cells surrounding the eyes.

Researchers don’t know for sure why some people develop autoimmune disorders, but they suspect that a combination of genetics and environmental factors, such as exposure to a viral trigger, are involved, says the National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases.

With Graves’ disease, the immune system manufactures an antibody called thyrotropin receptor antibody (TRAb) that activates the thyrotropin receptor (TSHR) in thyroid cells, causing overproduction of thyroid hormones, which results in hyperthyroidism, according to Mayo Clinic.

When it comes to TED, researchers believe this same antibody may be drawn to the tissue surrounding the eyes, because those tissues contain proteins that appear similar to those in the thyroid gland.

The relationship between Graves’ disease and TED is not entirely understood, but research published in Frontiers in Endocrinology in November 2020 suggests that Graves’ disease may somehow lead to TED, although the exact mechanism is unclear. In cases where TED appears first, the person is often later found to have Graves’ disease.

Risk Factors for Thyroid Eye Disease

Here are some of the risk factors that increase your chances of developing Graves’ ophthalmopathy.

Graves’ disease About one in three people with Graves’ disease develop thyroid eye disease, according to the NIDDK.

Female sex Women are up to six times more likely than men to develop thyroid eye disease.

Cigarette smoking Smokers are twice as likely to develop Graves' disease, and smokers with Graves' disease are more than seven times more likely to develop TED than nonsmokers, according to EyeRounds.org. In addition, smokers tend to have a longer duration of the active phase of TED.

A family member with thyroid eye disease If you have a family member with TED you're more likely to develop it, per NORD.

Radioactive iodine treatment for hyperthyroid Radioactive iodine is commonly used to treat hyperthyroid, including hyperthyroid caused by Graves’ disease. It can raise the risk that TED will develop or will get worse in those who already have it, according to the American Thyroid Association.

Low blood levels of selenium Some studies have suggested that having a selenium deficiency may raise the risk of thyroid eye disease. It has also been reported that tobacco use may result in selenium deficiency. Selenium is a mineral that occurs naturally in soil and is present in a broad range of foods.

How Is Thyroid Eye Disease Diagnosed?

Thyroid eye disease is diagnosed on the basis of symptoms, a person’s medical history, and a variety of tests and examinations, including the following.

Eye exams In addition to performing a complete ophthalmic evaluation, medical providers look for the typical signs and symptoms of early thyroid eye disease, including blurred vision, eye discomfort, dryness and redness, excess tearing and sensitivity to light. These are all nonspecific findings, however, and may be found in many other eye conditions. In more advanced TED, a person may have eyelid swelling and malpositioning, bulging eyes, and double vision, all of which are visible clinically. Eye muscle enlargement may also be detected clinically, but is usually best detected with imaging studies.

Endocrinology consultation If a person has no known history of Graves’ disease, they are usually referred to an endocrinologist for a consultation.

Scans A computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan might be ordered so that doctors can visualize your eye muscles and other soft tissues.

Blood tests Blood tests may be performed to check your thyroid hormone and antibody levels.

Many people with TED report seeing several different doctors before getting a diagnosis. This may be due to the relative rarity of the disease, and the fact that many of its symptoms overlap with other eye diseases.

Prognosis for Thyroid Eye Disease

According to the American Thyroid Association, thyroid eye disease is usually mild and gets better on its own over time, particularly if the underlying thyroid abnormalities are addressed.

Some people with TED have permanent changes to their eyes once the condition has reached its inactive phase. For those people, a variety of types of surgery can restore the eyes to more normal appearance and function.

Although the changes to the eyes caused by Graves’ ophthalmopathy can cause vision loss, according to the Kellogg Eye Center at University of Michigan Health, very few people with TED go on to develop blindness.

Duration of Thyroid Eye Disease

The active phase of TED generally lasts from six months to two years, after which the disease enters an inactive phase. Some symptoms may persist at this point, but they generally won’t progress or get worse.

Not everyone follows this disease course. According to NORD, some people experience repeated episodes of symptom worsening, followed by periods of improvement.

Treatment and Medication Options for Thyroid Eye Disease

Thyroid eye disease often resolves on its own, but some people will need specific treatments to alleviate their symptoms. A 2022 consensus statement by the American Thyroid Association and the European Thyroid Association lays out current recommendations for treatment.

The types of doctors who treat thyroid eye disease are subspecialty ophthalmologists known as neuro-ophthalmologists and oculoplastic surgeons.

It’s important to note that treatment for Graves’ disease will not improve symptoms of TED in people who have both conditions. Graves’ disease is usually treated by an endocrinologist who specializes in thyroid conditions, and while treatment of the underlying Graves' disease is extremely important, an eye specialist is usually required to directly treat any associated ophthalmopathy.

Medication Options

Medicines used to treat Graves’ ophthalmopathy include:

Eye drops Lubricating drops may help relieve dryness and grittiness in the eyes.

Lubricating gel A lubricant eye gel can be applied to the eye at night to prevent the cornea (outer layer of the eye) from drying out during sleep.

Steroids Treatment with intravenous glucocorticoids, such as prednisone or hydrocortisone may reduce swelling in the eyes.

Other medicines Drugs that act on the immune system, such as rituximab (Rituxan), and tocilizumab (Actemra) can sometimes provide relief for people in whom steroids are ineffective.

Selenium Some doctors recommend taking supplements of the mineral selenium as a low-risk way of possibly improving symptoms.

Tepezza The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the first drug specifically for the treatment of thyroid eye disease, teprotumumab (Tepezza), in 2020. Teprotumumab works by blocking the activity of the protein insulin-like growth factor-1, which is thought to play a role in the development of the disease. A single course of teprotumumab therapy — eight doses, three weeks apart — costs about $300,000.

If a person has stable double vision (meaning their eye misalignment is not changing over time), prescription prism glasses can often restore normal, single vision. If TED is in an active phase and diplopia is changing, then blocking one eye (with a patch or opaque spectacle lens) may be used as a temporary fix until symptoms stabilize. In severe cases, prism glasses may be insufficient, and surgery may be required.

Surgery

Sometimes surgical options are needed to manage the symptoms of thyroid eye disease. These may include the following.

Orbital decompression surgery In this type of surgery, orbital fat, and sometimes both bone and fat, are removed from the eye socket to create more space for swollen tissues and enable your eyes to return to their normal position. Your doctor might recommend this type of surgery if you have significant eye bulging or if symptoms of the disease are threatening your eyesight.

Eye muscle surgery The symptoms of TED can cause muscles around the eyes to become shortened or overly tight, which can pull the eyes out of alignment and restrict eye movement, causing double vision. Surgery to relocate the muscles may help to reduce or eliminate the problem.

Eyelid surgery Some people with Graves’ ophthalmopathy have difficulty closing their eyelids, which can lead to tearing and irritation. Eyelid surgery that repositions the lids may help.

Radiation Therapy

Rarely, doctors use radiation therapy to target the muscles and tissues around the eyes. According to the Mayo Clinic, this treatment may be recommended if your eye problems are getting worse, and other treatments aren’t effective. Some research also shows radiation therapy may be useful in minimizing the dose and duration of steroid treatment.

Home Remedies

Some home remedies to ease the symptoms of Graves’ ophthalmopathy include:

Elevate the head of your bed Keep your head higher than the rest of your body while you sleep to reduce the pressure on your eyes.

Cool compresses Apply cool or cold compresses to your eyes to relieve symptoms.

Complications of Thyroid Eye Disease

In rare cases, Graves’ ophthalmopathy can cause vision loss. This can occur in two different ways, as a consequence of symptoms of the disease.

Optic nerve compression Normally, the optic nerve relays messages from the eye to the brain, resulting in what we perceive as vision. Inflammation in the orbit (eye socket) can cause swelling of the extraocular eye muscles and orbital fat compartment, which leads to compression of the optic nerve. This complication can cause direct injury to the optic nerve, potentially leading to vision loss.

Corneal ulcer When the eye is pushed forward, you may have trouble closing your eyelids. Inability to blink properly and fully close the lids can lead to chronic drying, resulting in infection and ulceration of the cornea, the domed, clear, outermost part of the eye. Corneal ulceration can result in scarring and permanent vision loss, particularly if the scar is centrally located. Treatment of this severe complication usually requires a surgical procedure such as corneal transplantation.

Research and Statistics: Who Has Thyroid Eye Disease?

The exact number of people with thyroid eye disease is not known, but the disorder is estimated to affect 16 per 100,000 women in the general population, and 2.9 per 100,000 men in the general population, according to NORD. Though the disease is more common among women, it tends to be more severe among the men who develop it.

The median age at diagnosis is 43 years, per the Cleveland Clinic.

Research on the prevalence of TED among people of different races and ethnicities has had mixed results, with some showing Caucasians have a higher risk than other races, some showing people of Asian origin have a higher risk than Caucasians, and some showing no effect of race or ethnicity on the level of risk for thyroid eye disease.

Conditions Related to Thyroid Eye Disease

Some people with Graves’ ophthalmopathy may develop other autoimmune conditions, either at the same time as they have TED, or at a different time in their life.

According to a retrospective review, people with TED should be screened for other autoimmune conditions, especially vitiligo. Other conditions mentioned in the review include systemic sclerosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, myasthenia gravis, Sjögren's syndrome, and rheumatoid arthritis.

Resources for Thyroid Eye Disease

If you’ve been diagnosed with Graves’ ophthalmopathy, you should know that there are many resources to help you through your journey. Here are some of our favorites.

American Thyroid Association

The American Thyroid Association is a professional medical society with more than 1,700 members from 70 countries around the world. It offers educational information about different types of thyroid diseases, including Graves’ ophthalmopathy, for patients, as well as a “Find a Specialist” listing.

National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK)

The NIDDK is part of the National Institutes of Health (NIH), a federally funded medical research agency in the United States. The institute conducts and supports medical research and circulates health information to the public. Its site offers in-depth information about Graves’ disease.

Graves’ Disease and Thyroid Foundation (GDATF)

GDATF connects people with Graves’ disease and thyroid eye disease to a caring community. You can locate a support group in your area or register for upcoming webinars and other events.

Listen to Your Eyes

Horizon Therapeutics (maker of Tepezza), offers support and education for people living with thyroid eye disease, as well as a locator service for finding a TED specialist. Its Facebook page provides an easy means of communication.

oneGRAVESvoice

This online educational and social platform offers information and ways to connect for people living with Graves’ disease and thyroid eye disease.
