1. Understanding Wrist Tendon and Ligament Injuries

The wrist is a complex joint made up of eight small carpal bones, multiple tendons, ligaments, nerves and blood vessels. Common injuries include:

[for Strains](https://glycometrix.snaketongueofficial.com/)

Tendon strains and tendinopathy• Overuse injuries (e.g., repetitive wrist extension in typing or sports)• Micro-tears leading to inflammation and pain

Ligament sprains and tears• Trauma (e.g., falls onto an outstretched hand)• Chronic instability if left untreated

Typical symptoms:

Localized pain that worsens with movement

Swelling, tenderness, or bruising

Reduced grip strength or range of motion

Without proper care, acute strains and sprains can evolve into chronic pain or joint instability, sometimes prompting surgical intervention.

3. What Is PRP and How Does It Work?

Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) harnesses the body's own healing power:

A small sample of your blood is drawn.

It's spun in a centrifuge to concentrate platelets.

The platelet-rich layer (200–800% higher concentration than baseline) is injected into the injured area under ultrasound guidance.

Platelets release growth factors (e.g., PDGF, TGF-β, VEGF) that:

Stimulate cell proliferation

Enhance collagen production

Promote new blood vessel formation

Modulate inflammation

The goal: accelerate healing of tendon or ligament micro-tears without introducing foreign substances.

While much PRP research focuses on knee osteoarthritis and elbow tendinopathy, emerging studies support its use for wrist and hand tendon/ligament conditions:

A 2021 pilot study found that PRP injections reduced pain and improved function in patients with chronic wrist extensor tendinopathy.

Case series in scapholunate ligament injuries have reported improved stability and reduced pain scores at 6-month follow-up.

Systematic reviews on tendon injuries suggest PRP often outperforms corticosteroids for long-term relief, though results vary by condition and PRP preparation.

Key takeaways:

PRP appears safe, with minimal risk of adverse reaction.

Clinical improvement may continue for 3–6 months post-injection.

Best outcomes seen in partial tears and early tendinopathy rather than full-thickness ruptures.

Considerations

Cost: Often not covered by insurance; out-of-pocket expenses range from $500–$1,500 per session.

Delayed relief: Benefits may take weeks to months as tissue remodels.

Not a cure-all: Severe complete tears and chronic instability may still require surgery.

6. Is Wrist Surgery Avoidable?

PRP may help you avoid or delay surgery if:

You have partial tendon or ligament tears

You've failed conservative care but aren't facing structural collapse

You prefer a natural, regenerative approach and accept gradual improvement

However, surgery remains the gold standard when:

There's a full-thickness tear of a critical ligament (e.g., scapholunate)

Instability threatens long-term joint health

Conservative and biologic therapies have been exhausted

A tailored approach—combining immobilization, PT and PRP—can often reduce surgical rates for select patients. Shared decision-making with your surgeon or sports medicine specialist is essential.
